# Rituals, not gadgets, earn daily opens

DreamCRM should build an auto-prepared morning huddle first, dental-aware logs and renewal clocks second, a writing assistant that never sees patient data third, a small chairside toolbox fourth, and the 3D-printing library last, if at all. That order follows the evidence: the huddle is the only daily dental ritual with multi-source support and a paid product built around it, and sterilizer monitoring is the only candidate with an authoritative every-load, every-week cadence. The other three sets rest on vendor claims, analogues from medicine, or no usage data at all. The whole evidence base is thin: the research found no first-hand forum posts from dental staff, no daily-active-use figure for any dental product, and no review in which staff call a software feature enjoyable, so the ranking measures how well each habit is documented, not proven demand. Five legal lines set the scope: no patient information reaches the AI until a business associate agreement (BAA) exists, calculators stay inside the FDA's clinician-facing decision-support criteria, procedure codes wait for the ADA's commercial CDT licence, reminders never promise compliance, and most shared print files carry non-commercial licences. Inside those lines the first two builds need no AI and no reference library to maintain, which suits a very small team selling one $200 plan.

## Dental teams return to rituals, and no source documents enjoyment

Public sources give only fragments of the dental day. Hygienists describe about one patient an hour, seven or eight a day, opening with room setup, a schedule review and the team huddle; these are individual blog accounts, not benchmarks ([Dimensions of Dental Hygiene](https://dimensionsofdentalhygiene.com/article/standards-patient-scheduling/), [Ultradent](https://blog.ultradent.com/day-in-the-life-of-a-dental-hygienist)). The office manager carries insurance verification, claims, credentialing, HR, and OSHA and HIPAA paperwork while "the phone is ringing off the hook" ([DentistryIQ](https://www.dentistryiq.com/front-office/article/14274879/for-exhausted-dental-office-managers-help-is-out-there)). The research found no hour-by-hour account for the owner dentist, the associate, the assistant or the front desk, and nothing that counts how many programs each role keeps open.

**The one shared daily ritual is the morning huddle**: a 10-to-15-minute meeting of doctors, clinical staff and front office before the first patient ([Dental Economics](https://dentaleconomics.com/practice/article/14182976/how-a-daily-morning-meeting-could-transform-your-dental-practice), [Planet DDS](https://www.planetdds.com/blog/effective-morning-huddles-to-kickstart-your-day/)). Its prevalence is poorly measured. A Henry Schein One customer survey, reported second-hand and undated, found **82% hold regular huddles and more than 40% struggle to hold one every morning** because of time and information gathering ([Oral Health Group](https://www.oralhealthgroup.com/blogs/the-morning-huddle-just-getting-the-basics-right/)); an older Dentaltown poll found about 60% held none ([Dentaltown](https://www.dentaltown.com/blog/post/24835/why-morning-huddles-still-matter-in-dental-practices)). Both numbers are weak. What the sources agree on is the obstacle: a sample agenda has the team asking why a lab case was missing on the day of the appointment, and lack of preparedness is the usual reason huddles drag ([Burkhart](https://www.burkhartdental.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/AM-Huddle-Agenda.pdf), [Curve Dental](https://www.curvedental.com/blog/dental-morning-huddles)).

The best-evidenced pain in the day is one DreamCRM already addresses. Manual eligibility checks average 12 minutes each, according to a vendor relaying the 2024 CAQH Index, and front-desk staff put them off in favour of easier tasks ([RevenueWell](https://www.revenuewell.com/article/dental-ai-insurance-verification-vs-manual-checks-which-saves-more-time), [Dentaltown](https://www.dentaltown.com/blog/post/7458/is-dental-insurance-eligibility-taking-too-much-time)). The lesson for new tools is that the gap is less a missing feature than a missing moment in the day when the team looks.

Professional software that people open by choice follows one pattern in the public record: a narrow, free utility that removes an annoying task. MDCalc says more than 65% of US physicians use it monthly; Doximity says its network covers more than 85% of US physicians and sits beside a free dialer and fax line ([MDCalc](https://www.mdcalc.com/media), [Barchart](https://www.barchart.com/story/news/1913565/doximity-announces-fourth-quarter-and-fiscal-year-2026-financial-results), [Doximity](https://blog.doximity.com/articles/doximity-s-workflow-tools-032086e1-a442-4b73-8307-c0759056524a)). Both figures are self-reported reach. Neither company publishes daily or per-user numbers, so even the strongest cases prove breadth, not a daily habit.

Game mechanics have less support still. A 2025 scoping review of gamified tools for healthcare workers found **12 qualifying studies out of 5,844** and called the empirical evidence lacking ([JMIR](https://preprints.jmir.org/preprint/70480)). A Fitbit study found leaderboards added about 370 steps a day on average while the most active users' activity dropped and stayed down ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10403254)). In a team of five to fifteen, a ranking is personal; that is a design inference, untested in dental offices.

On enjoyment the record is empty. No review surfaced in which dental staff say they look forward to a software feature; praise centres on speed and ease. The morale data point elsewhere: 54.1% of dental professionals and 60.6% of hygienists report burnout in a 2026 survey run by a staffing marketplace, and **43% of dental assistants feel valued by their employer, down from 72% in 2020**, in a self-selected DANB survey ([DentistryIQ](https://www.dentistryiq.com/sponsored/article/55371709/what-7900-dental-professionals-revealed-in-a-state-of-work-report), [DANB](https://www.danb.org/news-blog/detail/news/certification-offers-a-solution-to-dental-assistant-workforce-challenges-new-data-shows)). "Enjoy" in this market therefore means relief and recognition: fewer 8 a.m. surprises, less prep, a thank-you that gets seen. No source shows software delivering the second.

## The huddle and the sterilizer log carry the strongest daily evidence

Ranked by how well daily use is documented, the five candidates fall in this order.

| Rank | Tool set | Documented cadence | Strongest evidence | Where it is thin |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Team operations, carried by the huddle | Daily, whole team | Multi-source trade press on the ritual; a paid product anchored on it; user reviews | Prevalence figures conflict; lab, supply, recognition and training have no daily-use data |
| 2 | Dental-aware logs and reminders | Every load, weekly, then 120 days to five years | CDC, OSHA, state boards and the ADA on cadence; real OSHA fines | No data on paper versus software, lapse rates or demand |
| 3 | Writing and reference assistant | Unmeasured | ADA dentist survey; the ADA's own appeal-writing playbook | No staff survey; voice demand is for charting, which needs patient data |
| 4 | Doctor's toolbox | Unmeasured | Anesthetic arithmetic documented as error-prone | Dental app engagement "relatively low"; nothing on timers |
| 5 | 3D-printing library | Printer owners only | 17% ownership in 2022 | No demand, community or utility-printing evidence |

**Team operations ranks first because of the huddle, not because of the set.** Dental Intelligence, the best-known product here, anchors on a huddle view that assembles yesterday, today, tomorrow and next week with no report to run ([Dental Intelligence](https://intercom.help/dental-intel/en/articles/12002688-morning-huddle)). Its price is unpublished; aggregators list **$399 to $899 a month**, or $449 a month plus $1,500 setup ([Software Advice](https://www.softwareadvice.com/dental/dental-intelligence-profile/reviews/), [SoftwarePundit](https://www.softwarepundit.com/node/72)). An office manager wrote that "everything from our morning huddle to our follow-up system is taken care of," while other reviewers complained that huddle numbers did not match Open Dental ([Capterra](https://www.capterra.com/p/185878/Dental-Intelligence/reviews/), [G2](https://g2.com/products/dental-intelligence-dental-intelligence/reviews)). That is the whole of the demand evidence: small review samples and vendor documentation. The claim that huddle practices outproduce others by 30% comes from a vendor with no study behind it and deserves no weight ([Jarvis Analytics](https://www.jarvisanalytics.com/?p=1786)).

The rest of the set is weaker. Lab-case tracking is evidenced as a process, not a pain with a number on it: practice-management systems record cases but do not chase them (CareStack's module "can be used only to keep track of orders internally"), and staff check by hand one or two days before the seat appointment ([CareStack](https://carestack.com/dental-software/features/lab-case-management), [Dentally](https://help.dentally.com/en/articles/10205410-how-to-use-lab-status-to-track-lab-work)). No source gives a late-arrival rate or the cost of a rescheduled seat; the only figure, a 10% remake rate, is a lab vendor's illustrative assumption about a different failure ([Glidewell](https://glidewelldental.com/company/blog/how-to-reduce-crown-remake-rates-and-recover-lost-chair-time)). Supply ordering falls to one person, often an assistant ([CDA](https://www.cda.org/newsroom/2022/inventory-management-is-a-cost-saving-tool-for-dentists/)); the 44-minutes-per-order figure comes from a supplier's white paper that was not verified ([NJDA](https://www.njda.org/news-information/news-details/2025/07/29/the-supply-ordering-mistakes-costing-your-practice-thousands)). Recognition has strong evidence of need, since 40% of assistants who left a job cited feeling underappreciated, and none that software closes the gap ([DANB](https://www.danb.org/news-blog/detail/blog/what-dentists-should-know-about-retaining-dental-assistants)). Training has turnover behind it, with 23% of assistants and 29.7% of front-office staff changing employers in 2024, but nothing shows staff opening written procedures daily ([DentalPost](https://www.dentalpost.net/blog/what-the-latest-salary-survey-tells-us-about-dental-team-turnover/)).

**Dental-aware logs and reminders rank second, with the most certain cadence and the least evidence of desire.** CDC recommends a spore test on every sterilizer at least weekly, a mechanical check every load and a chemical indicator in every package ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/dental-infection-control/hcp/dental-ipc-faqs/sterilization-monitoring.html)). State guidance describes per-autoclave paper logs with initials, time and pass/fail for each cycle ([Indiana Department of Health](https://secure.in.gov/health/oral-health/files/2020-02-f-Monitor.pdf)). Behind that daily loop sit slower clocks on every person and machine: OSHA's annual bloodborne-pathogens training and exposure-plan update ([OSHA](https://osha.gov/Publications/osha3186.pdf)), a credentialing-database re-attestation **every 120 days** ([ADA](https://www.ada.org/credentialing)), DEA registration every three years ([ADA](https://www.ada.org/en/resources/practice/legal-and-regulatory/dea-registration)), continuing-education cycles that run from 30 hours in 24 months in Nebraska to 63 hours in three years in Washington ([Pennsylvania Department of State](https://www.dos.pa.gov:443/ProfessionalLicensing/Documents/50-State-Licensing-Comparison/Dentistry.pdf)), and x-ray inspections every three to five years depending on the state ([Virginia](https://www.vdh.virginia.gov/radiological-health/radiological-health/x-ray-machine-program/dental-x-ray-machines/), [Texas](https://www.dshs.texas.gov/es/node/27631), [Utah](https://deq.utah.gov/waste-management-and-radiation-control/inspections-registrations-and-fees-x-ray-program)). Misses cost money: OSHA fined a Texas dentist $5,900 for late staff training, and a lapsed re-attestation can end an insurer contract ([Dentaltown](https://www.dentaltown.com/blog/post/8610/osha-fines-north-tx-dentist-5900-for-lack-of-timely-staff-training), [Anthem](https://providernews.anthem.com/dentalbc/articles/credentialing-with-caqh-proview-23976)).

What is missing is any measure of want. Template sellers offer spore-test logs and closing checklists, which shows that vendors perceive demand, not that practices adopt ([MangoApps](https://www.mangoapps.com/templates/inspections/dental-sterilizer-biological-spore-monitoring-log), [Connecteam](https://connecteam.com/templates/dental-office-end-of-day-checklist/)). No source says what share of practices still use binders, how often a licence or a log lapses, or that any team asked for an app. Existing vendors cluster in OSHA and HIPAA training and mostly price by quote; the research found no product that unifies licences, CE, equipment and sterilization logs, though review sites were not read, so that gap is unconfirmed ([Compliancy Group](https://compliancy-group.com/dental), [Rectangle Health](https://www2.rectanglehealth.com/dental-compliance-software-solution/)). This set ranks below the huddle because it is duty, not choice. It ranks above the rest because it is the one tool a clinical assistant would touch every working day, and assistants are the role with the least reason to open the app now (an inference from the product's current feature list, not a sourced finding).

## The assistant, toolbox and printer library stand on thinner ground

**The assistant ranks third: the documented uses are writing, and the documented voice demand is charting.** In the ADA's mid-2026 panel of 552 private-practice dentists, **43.3% use AI for at least one task**, led by imaging at 22.8%; among those planning to adopt, the top wishes are charting and note-taking (34.8%) and insurance verification (32.6%), and 82.6% do not plan to use AI for treatment recommendations ([ADA Health Policy Institute](https://www.ada.org/resources/research/health-policy-institute/dental-practice-research/dentists-ai-usage-and-attitudes)). The survey covers dentists only and does not measure general chatbot use; no staff survey was found. Voice has real traction in one place, perio charting and clinical notes, where Bola AI and Denti.AI each claim more than 10,000 users and Denti.AI charges $299 per location a month ([Denti.AI](https://www.denti.ai/blog-posts/best-dental-ai-scribes-in-2026-a-comprehensive-review)). Those are vendor claims from a self-interested comparison, and those products write patient findings into the chart, which DreamCRM's AI cannot touch today. The only smart-speaker precedent, DEXVoice, steers imaging software by Alexa command and is not a reference assistant ([Newswire](https://www.newswire.com/news/dexvoice-hands-free-software-supporting-the-dexis-imaging-suite-20767859)). No independent dental data exists on noise, accents or privacy in open operatories.

What remains is the work dental teams already hand to general chatbots: newsletters, job ads, text templates, tone rewrites, step-by-step office procedures and insurance appeal letters ([Dental Managers](https://www.dentalmanagers.com/blog/how-dental-office-managers-can-use-chatgbt/)). The ADA itself publishes a playbook on using AI for appeals while protecting patient privacy ([ADA News](https://adanews.ada.org/ada-pubplus/ebooks/the-denial-buster-playbook/)). That is a credible job list for a chat assistant and a poor case for a talking speaker. Frequency is unmeasured; the nearest signal is Doximity's claim that nearly half its active prescribers used its AI tools in a quarter, which is a company figure from medicine ([Barchart](https://www.barchart.com/story/news/1948381/doximity-q4-earnings-call-highlights)). The research also found no dedicated dental knowledge assistant on the market, which reads as an open niche or as a limit of the search; the notes cannot say which.

**The doctor's toolbox ranks fourth.** One job is well documented: anesthetic cartridge arithmetic, which hygienists and dentists do by hand and which the authors of a free open-source calculator call cognitively demanding and error-prone ([Today's RDH](https://www.todaysrdh.com/hygiene-clinician-and-mathematician-three-local-anesthetic-calculations-explained-and-simplified/), [MaxSafe](https://koreascience.or.kr/article/JAKO202617532508900.page)). Beyond that the signals point down. A 2023 survey of 206 dental apps concluded that engagement is relatively low, and the most visible chairside drug app holds 4.7 stars from about 1,200 ratings at **$2.99 a year** ([European Journal of General Dentistry](https://thieme-connect.de/products/ejournals/pdf/10.1055/s-0043-1761931.pdf), [App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/dental-drugs/id1010795610)). MDCalc's reach is monthly and in medicine. The research found nothing at all on how dental teams time curing, etching or sterilizer cycles, so timers have no evidence in either direction. Drug references and patient-education video belong to entrenched subscription vendors ([Wolters Kluwer](https://www.wolterskluwer.com/en/solutions/uptodate/pro/lexidrug-for-dentistry), [TrustRadius](https://www.trustradius.com/products/consult-pro-patient-education/pricing)).

**The 3D-printing library ranks last.** The only authoritative US figure dates from 2022: **17% of 277 surveyed dentists had a printer**, two-thirds of them for under two years, and non-owners said a lab makes their devices (44%) or the investment is too high (39%) ([ADA News](https://adanews.ada.org/ada-news/2023/may/ace-panel-report-finds-3d-printing-not-common-in-dental-offices-but-users-report-benefits/), [Becker's Dental](https://beckersdental.com/dental-technology/40971-only-17-of-private-practice-dentists-use-3d-printers-despite-benefits.html)). Ownership has very likely grown since, but no newer number exists, and the feature serves a minority of practices either way. The research found no survey of offices printing organizers or giveaways, no verified community size, and no demand evidence for a print log; the only companion tools are vendors' own return-on-investment calculators ([SprintRay](https://sprintray.com/roi-calculator/)).

## Five legal lines decide what each tool set can do

**Patient information and the AI.** Entering patient details into an AI product without a BAA is a disclosure to a third party, and a compliance attorney flags a staffer pasting patient details into a chatbot as a potentially reportable breach ([Holland & Hart](https://www.hollandhart.com/ai-chatbots-and-hipaa-what-healthcare-providers-need-to-know-1), [DrBicuspid](https://www.drbicuspid.com/dental-practice/legal-issues/podcast/15831225/ai-and-hipaa-in-dentistry-what-your-team-doesnt-know-can-hurt-you)). This shapes three sets. The huddle's patient rows are assembled by rules, not by the AI. The assistant uses placeholders such as [PATIENT NAME], the pattern appeal-writing guides already teach, and screens what staff type before it leaves ([findskill.ai](https://findskill.ai/blog/dental-insurance-preauthorization-letter-ai/)). Logs and renewal clocks describe staff and equipment, not patients, so they sit outside this limit. The exception is the employee health paperwork OSHA requires, the sharps-injury log and hepatitis B forms, which is best left out of a first version; that is a judgment, not a sourced finding ([OSHA](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20120927234404/http:/www.osha.gov/SLTC/bloodbornepathogens/standards.html)).

**The medical-device line.** The FDA's revised guidance of January 29, 2026 excludes decision-support software from device status only when it meets four criteria: it analyses no images or signals, it displays or analyses medical information, it supports a clinician's decision, and the clinician can independently review the basis. Software that gives recommendations to patients is a device, and time-critical outputs remain a concern ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/clinical-decision-support-software), [Cooley](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-01-20-automation-bias-and-clinical-practice-fda-makes-incremental-updates-to-clinical-decision-support-software-guidance)). The toolbox therefore shows its formula, inputs and source on every result, stays out of the patient portal, and issues no urgent directives. MDCalc and PerioCalc model the posture: professional use only, "not certified as a medical device," educational ([MDCalc](https://www.mdcalc.com/disclaimer), [PerioCalc](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/id6470758932)). Published maximum-dose values differ by source, so a calculator names the one it uses ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30275134/)). The assistant answers reference questions with citations and declines patient-specific treatment advice, which also matches what dentists say they want. The research did not check the criteria against the guidance text and none of this is a legal opinion, so counsel should confirm before any calculator ships. Whatever the tool says, the dentist stays liable for an error they fail to catch ([AGD](https://issuu.com/agddentist/docs/_12_dec_book/s/61835540)).

**CDT codes.** Any commercial product that uses, copies or distributes the ADA's procedure codes needs a commercial licence; royalties are unpublished and processing takes two to four weeks, sometimes two months. Dentists and their teams need no licence to use the codes themselves ([ADA](https://www.ada.org/en/publications/ada-store-products/licensing-for-commercial-users)). Until DreamCRM holds a licence, neither the assistant nor the toolbox looks up, suggests or prints codes; staff type their own into a letter. This is a reading of the ADA's page, which does not mention AI.

**Compliance claims.** CDC's sterilization schedule is a recommendation, record retention is left to the states, and CDC sets no waterline testing interval and points to the unit's manufacturer ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/dental-infection-control/hcp/dental-ipc-faqs/sterilization-monitoring.html), [ADA](https://www.ada.org/en/resources/ada-library/oral-health-topics/dental-unit-waterlines)). The research could not verify CPR renewal, eyewash, fire-extinguisher or HIPAA risk-analysis intervals at all. A reminder product therefore lets the practice set every interval, offers defaults that cite their authority and date, and says "we remind; you remain responsible." The largest vendor in the category disclaims OSHA certification, full regulatory coverage and legal advice in its own support pages ([Compliancy Group](https://compliancygroup.freshdesk.com/support/solutions/articles/48001246812-what-do-osha-s-medical-and-dental-osha-solutions-cover-)). No case law or regulator guidance on vendor liability was researched.

**Print files and printed devices.** Dental models on maker sites commonly carry non-commercial or no-derivatives Creative Commons licences, which bar use "primarily intended for or directed towards commercial advantage" ([MakerWorld](https://makerworld.com/en/models/1019257-human-teeth-model), [Creative Commons](https://wiki.creativecommons.org/NonCommercial_interpretation)). A paid app cannot safely host them; it hosts original or permissively licensed designs, or links out. The FDA regulates what comes off the printer when the output is a medical device, not the printer, and printable denture-base resins are Class II devices ([Pew](https://www.pewtrusts.org/-/media/assets/2022/07/fdas-regulatory-framework-for-3d-printing-of-medical-devices.pdf), [FDA](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cdrh/cfdocs/cfTPLC/tplc.cfm?id=EBI)). A library stays with non-device items and never calls a resin or setting "validated."

## Build the huddle first and the printer library last

The build order splits the five sets where the evidence splits them, and every step ships behind its own opt-in switch.

| Order | Build | Why here | Limits that shape it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Auto-prepared huddle with a lab-case arrival check | Best-documented daily ritual; reuses synced data; reaches the whole team | Patient rows assembled by rules, never the AI; every figure shows its source and sync time; no rankings |
| 2 | Sterilizer and spore-test logs, open and close checklists, then licence, CE, DEA, credentialing and equipment clocks | Most certain cadence; brings in clinical assistants; feeds the huddle | Practice sets every interval; defaults cite authority and date; reminds, never certifies; no state rules engine yet |
| 3 | Writing assistant in the existing chat: appeals, job ads, procedures, training outlines | Documented uses; the persona already exists | No patient identifiers; no CDT codes without a licence; no treatment advice; voice deferred |
| 4 | Small toolbox: a transparent anesthetic cartridge calculator and links to ADA guidance | One documented job; low cost; a convenience, not a habit | Clinician-facing only; formula, inputs and source shown; counsel review first |
| 5 | 3D printing, deferred; at most a vendor-neutral cost-per-print calculator | Minority of practices; no demand evidence | Original or permissively licensed files only; non-device items; no "validated" settings |

Step one is cheap because most of a huddle's content is already synced: the day's schedule, unconfirmed visits, eligibility not yet checked, forms not yet signed, follow-ups due. The new record is the lab case (a lab, a due date and the seat appointment it belongs to), so the page answers "will it be here?" two days out, the check staff now make by hand. The failure mode is known in advance: reviewers abandon trust when a huddle number disagrees with the practice-management system, so the page sticks to what DreamCRM holds first-hand, stamps every synced row with its age, and leaves production scoreboards to analytics vendors. It also has to work where no meeting happens, as a brief each person reads on a phone or a printout.

Step two gives clinical staff a reason to open the app and gives the huddle more rows, such as a spore test due today or a DEA registration that renews in 60 days. It starts with the loop that is certain (the load log, the weekly spore test per sterilizer, CDC's cited steps after a positive result, open and close checklists) and adds person and equipment clocks as dated entries the practice types in. A state-by-state rules database is the expensive part and waits for demand. A shared "we're low on this" list and a private thank-you note fit here as small additions; neither has evidence that justifies more, and the leaderboard research argues against any ranked version of recognition.

Step three reuses the chat persona. Its job list comes from documented behaviour, and drafting procedures and training outlines is the practical form of "staff training" for a team this small, where dedicated training software lists at $99 to $295 a month ([eLearning Industry](https://elearningindustry.com/trainual-pricing)). Voice waits: the demand on record is for charting, which needs patient data and write access to the chart. Step four is a convenience that costs little; the ADA's antibiotic-prophylaxis guidance has changed enough (clindamycin is no longer a recommended alternative) that the toolbox links to the ADA page instead of caching it ([ADA](https://www.ada.org/en/resources/ada-library/oral-health-topics/antibiotic-prophylaxis)). Timers go in only if they are nearly free to build.

The order also fits the price. Practices pay $399 or more a month for huddle analytics and a reported $299 for a compliance bundle, each above DreamCRM's entire plan, while the chairside drug app charges $2.99 a year. Folding the first two categories into a $200 plan strengthens the price; the toolbox cannot. These prices come from aggregators and conflict with each other, so they anchor direction, not magnitude. Two events reorder the list: a signed BAA moves the assistant up, because charting and note-taking is the top wish of dentists planning to adopt AI, and a CDT licence opens coding help.

## Conclusion

The five candidates are not five products. The evidence points to one daily page, the huddle, and four feeders: logs and clocks supply its due items, lab cases and supply flags supply its warnings, the assistant turns a row into a letter, and the toolbox hangs beside it as a convenience. Building them as separate destinations would recreate the problem DreamCRM has now, a set of capable tools with no moment in the day when anyone looks.

Two tasks that involve no engineering carry more weight than any single feature. A BAA with the AI vendor is the one change that would reorder this list. And the missing first-hand evidence is cheap to collect: because every feature is opt-in, each switch is a demand measurement that no survey in this research supplies, and a dozen conversations with office managers and assistants would test the ranking before step three is built.
